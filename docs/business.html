For finance, audit and operations

The data arrives. The evidence does not.

A figure reaches a report having passed through four systems, two vendors and a spreadsheet. Reconstructing how it got there is an email search months later. This page is about what changes when the trail travels with the number.

The cost of reconstructing

Nothing here is a failure of controls. The invoice was approved, the client was screened, the model was asked. The difficulty is that each step leaves its evidence in a different system, in a different format, under a different owner — and assembling them afterwards is manual work performed under time pressure, usually by whoever is least able to refuse.

Three costs follow, and finance teams will recognise all of them.

Audit preparation Evidence is gathered retrospectively, from people and inboxes rather than from the record.
Counterparty trust A figure sent outside the company carries no evidence, so the recipient either trusts you or repeats the work.
Silent gaps A missing approval looks exactly like a present one until somebody goes looking.

Two kinds of step, and why it matters commercially

This distinction is what separates a provenance trail that is worth something from one that is decorative. Every step in a pipeline is one of two kinds, and they carry different weight as evidence.

Verifiable

You can re-run it

Totalling a column. Converting a currency. Matching an invoice against a purchase order. The same inputs always produce the same answer.

The record keeps the calculation, its inputs and its result. A counterparty fetches them and runs it again.

  • No signature required
  • Nobody has to be believed
Attested

Someone signs for it

Reading figures off a scan. Asking a model to judge something. A human approval. Run it twice and the answer may differ.

So the operator records the inputs, the method and the result, and signs it.

  • The signature is the evidence
  • Weight depends on whose key it is
Most of what a finance pipeline does is reproducible. Those steps stop requiring trust entirely — which is the part that is genuinely new.

A real pipeline mixes the two. Being able to state which parts a counterparty can check and which rest on an attestation is more useful than a single signature over a finished report, where everything inside carries the same unexamined weight.

What an auditor does with it

  1. Start from the figureThe published number, and the signed statement that accompanies it.
  2. Request the inputsEach disclosed piece must match a reference already present in the evidence, so nothing can be substituted afterwards.
  3. Re-run what can be re-runThe calculations either agree or they do not. No judgement required.
  4. Check signatures on the restWhich key signed, and was it authorised for that role.
  5. Read the gapsA step with nothing behind it is visible, rather than indistinguishable from a step that was completed.

What it does not solve

A provenance system that claimed to settle everything would be overselling. Drawing the line precisely is more useful than drawing it generously.

Honest limits

  • A signature attributes a claim to a key, not to a person. Connecting keys to people is a governance problem, not a cryptographic one.
  • No amount of checking shows that an extraction read the document correctly. Only comparing against the original does.
  • Re-running later calculations cannot repair a bad reading at the start. It shows the calculations were faithful to whatever they were given.
  • It records what happened. It does not decide whether what happened was acceptable.

Where this is today

MSD is a new and open protocol, and it is early. Signing and verifying structured data, content-addressed references between records, and embedding into documents you already send all work today; the SDK and key tooling are public. The visual tooling that assembles a graph from dropped files is still being built.

We would rather state the stage plainly than imply an adoption story that does not exist yet.

Data provenance, explained

Some of it you can check yourself. The rest you take on trust.

Provenance systems promise to tell you where a number came from. The useful question is sharper: which parts of that story can you verify independently, and which parts rest on someone's word? Most tools blur the line. This one draws it.

The scenario

A delivery driver photographs an invoice. Software reads the fields off the photo. Those fields are reformatted, totalled, and rendered into a PDF report. The report goes to a customer, who files it. Months later an auditor asks a reasonable question:

This figure in your report — where did it come from?

Four handoffs sit between the photograph and the figure. The honest answer is not a single yes or no, because the four steps are not the same kind of thing.

Two kinds of step

Every step in a data pipeline falls into one of two categories, and the difference decides what an auditor can do about it.

Reproducible

Re-run it

Same input, same output, every time. Converting "1,190.00" into 119000 cents. Totalling a column. Rendering a template.

Record the function, the input and the output — each by a fingerprint of its content. An auditor fetches the function and the input, runs it, and compares.

  • No signature needed
  • No key to trust
  • No one to believe

Not reproducible

Someone signs for it

Reading text off a photograph. Asking a model for a judgement. Taking the photo in the first place. Run it twice and you may get two answers.

Replay proves nothing here. So the operator records what went in, which model and settings, what came out — and signs it.

  • Signature is the evidence
  • Trust rests on the key
  • And on who vouched for it
For the reproducible steps, the auditor does not have to believe anyone. They can check. That is the part most provenance tools cannot offer.

What the record looks like

Everything is referred to by a fingerprint of its content rather than by a filename or a database row. A filename can point at different content tomorrow; a content fingerprint cannot. Two useful things follow.

Why fingerprints rather than filenames

You can refer to something without sending it. A report can point at a 4 MB photograph without carrying it. If the image is produced later, anyone can confirm it is the one that was referenced.

References never go stale. "The version of this calculation we used in September" stays meaningful next year, even after the code changes.

What the auditor actually does

  1. Start with what arrivedA PDF and a signed statement from the company. The statement points at the report data, the calculation, the reading step and the original photograph — all by fingerprint. The PDF can be checked immediately, without any of the upstream material.
  2. Ask for the piecesThe company discloses the referenced fragments. Each must match a fingerprint already present in the evidence, so nothing can be quietly substituted after the fact.
  3. Re-run what can be re-runReplay the reformatting and the rendering. Compare the results. No trust involved — this either matches or it does not.
  4. Check signatures on the restThe photograph, the reading step, the publication. Here the question becomes whose key is this, and were they authorised to sign for that.
  5. Notice what is absentA reference with nothing behind it is a visible hole, not a silent one. You can see precisely which link in the chain is missing.

The division, in one table

Reproducible stepNon-reproducible step
ExamplesReformatting, totalling, renderingOCR, model inference, photo capture
What is recordedFunction, input, output — by fingerprintInputs, model, settings, output — then signed
Auditor's moveRun it againVerify the signature, judge the signer
Trust requiredNoneThe key, and who vouched for it

A real pipeline is always a mixture. Being able to say which parts are checkable and which are attested is the point — compare it with a single signature over a finished report, where everything inside is equally opaque.

What this still cannot tell you

  • A signature attributes a claim to a key, not to a person. Connecting a key to a real identity is a separate problem, and a social one.
  • No amount of fingerprinting shows that the OCR read the invoice correctly. Only looking at the photograph answers that.
  • Re-running the later calculations cannot repair a bad reading at the start. It can only show the calculations were faithful to it.

A provenance system that claimed to settle those questions would be overselling. Drawing the line precisely is more useful than drawing it generously.
